# Problem

Artifacts are one of the few things in modern infrastructure that are inherently portable. A container image, NuGet package, Python wheel, or binary is fundamentally just immutable content plus metadata. Once produced, it should be trivially replicable, mirrorable, cacheable, and distributable across arbitrary locations. In practice, however, most CI/CD systems do not treat artifacts this way. Instead, they tightly couple artifact behaviour to specific registry implementations, cloud vendors, authentication flows, network assumptions, and deployment environments. The artifact stops being treated as portable data and instead becomes inseparable from the mechanism currently storing it.

This creates a form of operational lock-in that extends beyond vendor preference. Pipelines, deployment logic, permissions, environment configuration, and infrastructure automation all become written around specific artifact destinations such as ECR, CodeArtifact, Docker Hub, GitHub Container Registry, Artifactory, or Quay. As a result, changing where artifacts live becomes disproportionately expensive relative to what is actually occurring technically: copying immutable files from one location to another. Even simple transitions often require CI rewrites, endpoint changes, permission restructuring, deployment updates, and assumptions scattered throughout automation stacks. The operational burden becomes dominated by migration complexity rather than artifact movement itself.

A secondary issue is that artifact intelligence is fragmented throughout pipelines. Detection logic, upload behaviour, registry selection, and publishing rules are often duplicated across repositories and CI implementations. Pipelines become collections of bespoke upload assumptions.
